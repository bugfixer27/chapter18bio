Overview · Mining the genome · Figure 18.1

A living fossil, read letter by letter.

The elephant shark (Callorhinchus milii) has a skeleton of cartilage and the slowest-changing genome of every vertebrate sequenced so far: a doorway into the distant past.

Its opposite: the tiger tail seahorse, whose males brood the young, has the fastest-evolving fish genome known. Comparing genomes like these is how we ask what made each species what it is.

Genome · genomics · bioinformatics

Everything it is, written in four letters.

A genome is an organism's complete set of genetic material. Genomics studies whole sets of genes and their interactions; bioinformatics applies computing to store and analyse the enormous data.

Watch the shark come apart into the one molecule that built it.

DNA · atom by atom

Two strands, one code.

Sugar–phosphate backbones outside (phosphorus orange, oxygen red); paired bases stacked inside: A–T, G–C. About 3.4 nm per turn, 2 nm across.

18.1

Reading the genome

1990 – 2003 · Human Genome Project

Twenty centres. Six countries. One book.

The Human Genome Project officially began in 1990 and was largely completed in 2003: an international, publicly funded consortium to sequence every base pair of a human genome.

Its biggest legacy was not the sequence but the machines it forced into existence: faster, cheaper sequencing.

3 billion base pairs

The helix coils into a chromosome.

A haploid set of human chromosomes holds about 3 billion base pairs. The goal of mapping any genome: the complete nucleotide sequence of every chromosome.

Shown here as a duplicated (metaphase) chromosome: two sister chromatids joined at the centromere, stained into bands.

Whole-genome shotgun · step 1 · Figure 18.2

Take many copies. Cut them at random.

In 1998 J. Craig Venter proposed the whole-genome shotgun approach: cut the DNA from many copies of a chromosome into overlapping fragments short enough to sequence.

Each copy breaks in different places, so the pieces overlap. That overlap is the whole trick.

Step 2 · clone

Clone every fragment.

The fragments are cloned in plasmids or other vectors, making enough of each to sequence.

Predict · the book's Visual Skills question

Why are the fragments shown scattered, not in order?

Right: the cut is random, so a fragment's position is lost. Only the overlaps between fragments can put the order back.

Step 3 · sequence

Read each fragment.

Every fragment is sequenced on its own. Three of them, straight from Figure 18.2:

CGCCATCAGTAGTCCGCTATACGAACGATACTGGT

Step 4 · order by overlap

Software stitches them back together.

Computer programs find where the end of one read matches the start of another, then line up thousands of overlapping reads into one continuous sequence.

…CGCCATCAGTCCGCTATACGATACTGGT…

Faster, cheaper

From thirteen years to a single day.

A 1980s lab read about 1,000 base pairs a day; by 2000, 1,000 a second; by 2018 the best machines read nearly 35 million a second. Sequencing by synthesis reads millions of ~300-bp fragments at once, with no cloning step.

The first human genome: 13 years and $100 million. In 2007 one person's: 4 months, about $1 million. By 2018: a day or less, about $1,000.

Metagenomics

Sequence a whole community at once.

In metagenomics, DNA from every species in an environmental sample is collected and sequenced together; software sorts the partial sequences into individual genomes.

No need to grow each species in the lab: most microbes can't be cultured at all.

2018 · the contents of cow rumens

913 genomes out of one soup.

Researchers sequenced DNA from cow rumens and assembled 913 microbial genomes, finding over 60,000 genes for carbohydrate digestion that had never been identified.

Takeaway · 18.1

  1. Shotgun: cut many copies at random, clone, sequence, order by overlaps.
  2. New methods sequence short fragments directly, millions at once.
  3. Metagenomics sequences a whole community's DNA together.
18.2

Bioinformatics

Centralized resources

Every genome, one search box.

The National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), created by the National Library of Medicine and the NIH, runs GenBank: a public database of every sequence submitted. Europe (EMBL), Japan (DDBJ) and China (BGI) keep matching archives.

Anyone can search it for a DNA sequence, a predicted protein, or a stretch of amino acids, and view solved 3-D protein structures.

Predict · your first bioinformatics move

You've sequenced a gene no one has studied. Fastest clue to its function?

Right: a program like BLAST aligns your sequence against millions of others. Similar sequence usually means similar function, because similar genes share ancestry.

BLAST · alignment

Line it up against everything ever sequenced.

BLAST slides your query along database sequences and scores the matches. A protein's predicted amino acid sequence is compared too: amino acids can match even where the DNA has drifted.

This is how most genes in a new genome are first given a likely function.

Understanding protein-coding genes

Where does a gene start?

Software scans a raw sequence for the signatures of genes: start and stop codons, RNA-splicing sites, and evidence that the region is actually transcribed into RNA.

A genome browser stacks this evidence as tracks: here, one gene's five exons with its RNA piling up over them.

Field note · Ciona robusta

Two sea squirts, one secret switch.

Compare the genomes of two Ciona species and most noncoding DNA has drifted apart, but some blocks stay nearly identical. Those conserved noncoding blocks often turn out to be enhancers: switches that control when a gene turns on.

That's how the notochord enhancer of Ciona's Brachyury gene was found and narrowed to 434 bp; each candidate is tested by electroporating reporter DNA into hundreds of embryos at once (Corbo, Levine & Zeller 1997).

Unknown genes

When nothing matches, break it.

For a gene with no known relative, researchers disable it and watch what goes wrong in the organism. Biochemical and functional studies fill in the rest.

The systems level

Genes work in networks.

Proteomics studies the full set of proteins a genome encodes (its proteome). Systems biology models the behaviour of whole systems from the interactions among their parts, like this map of yeast proteins, coloured by the cell function each cluster serves.

2003 · ENCODE

Most of the genome is read, somewhere, sometime.

The ENCODE project (Encyclopedia of DNA Elements) catalogued protein-coding genes, noncoding RNA genes and regulatory sequences. At least 75% of the human genome is transcribed at some point in at least one cell type.

It was extended to the nematode and the fruit fly (modENCODE).

Medicine

Cancer, read as a system.

The Cancer Genome Atlas compares the genomes of tumours with normal tissue, gene by gene, across many cancer types, finding shared networks of changes rather than single broken genes. Microarray chips test the expression of thousands of genes at once.

BLAST · query vs GenBank

Takeaway · 18.2

  1. Databases (GenBank) and tools (BLAST) let anyone compare sequences.
  2. Similar sequence suggests similar function; genes are found by their signals.
  3. Systems biology studies networks; ENCODE: ≥ 75% of DNA is transcribed.
18.3

Size is not complexity

Predict

Which has the largest genome?

Right: Paris japonica has about 149,000 Mb, roughly 50 times a human's 3,000 Mb.

Genome size · Table 18.1

Volume, drawn to scale.

Most bacteria and archaea have 1–6 million base pairs (Mb); most plants and animals over 100 Mb; humans about 3,000 Mb. Within each domain, genome size shows no systematic relationship to how complex the organism looks.

Each sphere's volume is proportional to its genome. The last one never fits the frame.

Predict

Who has more genes: you, or an ear of corn?

Right: corn has about 32,000 genes; humans fewer than 21,000, about as many as a 1-mm nematode worm.

Number of genes

More DNA doesn't mean more genes.

Free-living bacteria and archaea have 1,500–7,500 genes; unicellular fungi about 5,000; multicellular eukaryotes up to at least 40,000. The nematode (C. elegans, 100 Mb) has about 20,100 genes; the fruit fly (165 Mb) only about 14,000.

Gene density

A bacterium is almost all genes.

Each bar is one million base pairs. E. coli packs about 950 genes into it; a human, about 7. Mammals have the lowest gene density known: long introns and vast noncoding DNA between genes.

Alternative splicing

One gene, several proteins.

How do so few genes build a human? In vertebrates most genes can be spliced more than one way: alternative splicing keeps different combinations of exons, so one gene yields several different polypeptides.

Takeaway · 18.3

  1. Genome size doesn't track complexity; gene number doesn't track size.
  2. Humans: ~3,000 Mb, < 21,000 genes, ~7 genes per Mb.
  3. Alternative splicing gets many proteins from one gene.
18.4

What a genome holds

The human genome, end to end

Three billion letters, laid out.

Every colour is a kind of DNA, in its true proportion.

Predict

How much of it codes for proteins?

Right: exons are about 1.5%. The other 98.5% doesn't code for proteins, rRNAs or tRNAs.

Types of DNA sequences · Figure 18.5

1.5% codes for protein.

Only the exons are protein-coding. The rest was once called “junk DNA”, but much of it is conserved across species and is transcribed, which argues that it matters.

Noncoding, but not idle

Introns, switches and fossils.

About a quarter of the genome is introns and gene-related regulatory sequences. Between genes lies intergenic DNA, including pseudogenes (former genes that have accumulated mutations and no longer work) and 15% unique noncoding DNA.

Repetitive DNA

Over half of us is repeats.

Repetitive DNA is present in multiple copies. About three-quarters of it is transposable elements and related sequences (44% of the genome); the other 14% arose from errors in replication or recombination.

Simple sequence DNA · STRs

Stutters that make you unique.

Simple sequence DNA is many copies of a short unit in tandem. When the unit is 2–5 nucleotides it's a short tandem repeat (STR). The number of repeats varies from person to person, giving each a unique genetic profile, used in forensics.

Much simple sequence DNA sits at centromeres and telomeres, where it's structural. A third of the 14% is large-segment duplications (5–6%), copied from one region to another.

Next · the notebook

The biggest slice moves.

That 44% is made of DNA that copies itself from place to place. The first person to see it happen did so decades before anyone knew genes were DNA.

Takeaway · 18.4 (part 1)

  1. Exons: 1.5%; introns + regulatory: ~¼; the rest intergenic.
  2. Repetitive DNA ≈ half; transposable elements ≈ 44%.
  3. STRs (2–5-nt units) vary between people: genetic profiles.
18.4

Jumping genes

Barbara McClintock · Indian corn · Figure 18.6

Speckled kernels, moving genes.

Breeding corn in the 1940s and '50s, Barbara McClintock saw kernel colours change in ways that only made sense if pieces of DNA were moving into and out of the genes for pigment. She was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1983.

Each spot is a clone of cells where the element jumped back out and colour returned.

Into one kernel

Transposable elements.

Transposable elements move from one site in a cell's DNA to another. They're found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and they come in two kinds.

Transposons · Figure 18.7

Cut and paste. Or copy and paste.

A transposon moves through a DNA intermediate. An enzyme, transposase, cuts it out and inserts it at a new site (“cut and paste”) or copies it first and leaves the original behind (“copy and paste”).

Predict · Concept Check

Which kind of element always leaves a copy at the original site?

Right: a retrotransposon is copied into RNA first; the original stays put and a new DNA copy is inserted elsewhere.

Retrotransposons · Figure 18.8

Through RNA and back.

A retrotransposon is transcribed into an RNA intermediate; reverse transcriptase (which it encodes) turns the RNA back into DNA, and the new copy inserts at another site. The original never leaves.

That's why they pile up: every jump adds a copy.

Alu and L1

A tenth of you is one sequence.

Alu elements, about 300 nucleotides long, make up about 10% of the human genome; many are transcribed, though any function is unknown. L1 retrotransposons are about 17%; they rarely move, and may help regulate gene expression.

Takeaway · 18.4 (part 2)

  1. Transposons move by a DNA intermediate (transposase).
  2. Retrotransposons move by an RNA intermediate (reverse transcriptase) and always leave a copy.
  3. Alu ≈ 10%, L1 ≈ 17% of the human genome.
18.4

Gene families

Identical genes · Figure 18.9a

Hundreds of copies, side by side.

Most genes are single-copy, but multigene families are collections of identical or very similar genes. The ribosomal RNA genes repeat in tandem, each unit transcribed into one RNA later cut into 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs, separated by nontranscribed spacer DNA.

Why so many identical copies? A cell needs millions of ribosomes; one gene can't make rRNA fast enough. Each “tree” is one unit being transcribed by many polymerases at once.

Nonidentical genes · Figure 18.9b

Globins: two families, two chromosomes.

The α-globin family sits on chromosome 16, the β-globin family on chromosome 11. Scattered among the working genes are pseudogenes (ψ): copies that mutated into silence.

Hemoglobin

Two α-type chains, two β-type chains.

Each hemoglobin molecule is four polypeptides, each holding an iron-containing heme that carries O₂: two from the α family and two from the β family.

Predict

A fetus uses γ-globin instead of β. Why would it need a different hemoglobin?

Right: embryonic and fetal hemoglobins bind O₂ more strongly than adult hemoglobin, so oxygen moves from mother to fetus across the placenta.

A developmental switch

Different genes, different times.

ζ and ε are made in the embryo; α and the two γ genes (Gγ, Aγ) in the fetus; α and β (with a little δ) in the adult. The family's members differ just enough to suit each stage.

Curves are schematic: the order and timing of the switches, not exact amounts.

Takeaway · 18.4 (part 3)

  1. Multigene families: identical (rRNA, in tandem) or nonidentical (globins).
  2. α-globins on chromosome 16, β-globins on 11, with pseudogenes.
  3. Family members are expressed at different developmental stages.
18.5

Duplication and rearrangement

Whole chromosome sets

An accident that doubles everything.

Mutation is the raw material of genome evolution. An error in meiosis can add an entire extra set of chromosomes: polyploidy. One copy of each gene keeps the old job, so the spare copy is free to mutate, and occasionally gains a new function.

Field note · Ciona robusta

One copy where we have four.

Early vertebrates went through two rounds of whole-genome duplication. Ciona, whose lineage split off before them, keeps many developmental genes in single copy: one Hox cluster (split across two chromosomes) where mammals have four, one Brachyury.

That's part of why it's such a clean system for studying how a gene like Brachyury works (Dehal & Boore 2005).

Predict

Humans have 23 chromosome pairs; chimpanzees have 24. What most likely happened?

Right: human chromosome 2 matches chimp chromosomes 12 and 13 end to end, and still carries the evidence of the join.

Figure 18.10 · human chromosome 2

A fusion you can still see.

After the human and chimp lineages split, two ancestral chromosomes fused. Human chromosome 2 still has telomere-like sequences in its middle and the remains of a second centromere.

Figure 18.11 · human vs mouse

Same blocks, reshuffled.

Large blocks of genes on human chromosome 16 are found together on mouse chromosomes 7, 8, 16 and 17: the blocks stayed intact while the chromosomes around them were rearranged. Duplications and inversions arise from errors in meiotic recombination, and they seem to have accelerated about 100 million years ago, as mammals diversified.

Figure 18.12 · unequal crossing over

A misaligned swap copies a gene.

During prophase I, homologous chromosomes can pair slightly out of register, often where transposable elements give them matching sequence. A crossover there is unequal: one chromatid ends up with two copies of a gene, the other with none.

Errors during DNA replication (slippage) can also duplicate stretches of DNA.

Next

A spare copy is an experiment.

Takeaway · 18.5 (part 1)

  1. Polyploidy duplicates whole sets; spare copies can diverge.
  2. Human 2 = a fusion of two ancestral (chimp 12 + 13) chromosomes.
  3. Unequal crossing over: one chromatid gains a gene, the other loses it.
18.5

New genes from old

Genes with related functions

Read the globin families backwards.

Every α and β gene, pseudogenes included, descends from one ancestor.

Figure 18.13 · 450–500 million years

Duplicate. Mutate. Move. Repeat.

An ancestral globin gene duplicated about 450–500 million years ago. Both copies accumulated mutations, becoming the α and β lineages; transposition carried them to different chromosomes; further duplications and mutations gave today's families, pseudogenes included.

Genes with novel functions

Same shape, new job.

Lysozyme is an enzyme that helps protect animals against bacteria. α-Lactalbumin is a nonenzymatic protein that helps make milk in mammals. They're so similar in sequence and 3-D structure that they must be duplicated copies of one gene; one copy gained a new function.

Predict · Test Your Understanding

Two proteins share one domain but are otherwise unrelated. Most likely cause?

Right: domains are often encoded by separate exons, so an exon moved into another gene brings its domain with it.

Domains and exons

Proteins built from parts.

Proteins often have domains, discrete structural and functional regions, frequently encoded by different exons. Unequal crossing over can duplicate an exon within a gene: exon duplication.

Figure 18.14 · exon shuffling

TPA: a gene assembled from three others.

In exon shuffling, exons are mixed and matched between different genes. The gene for tissue plasminogen activator (TPA), which dissolves blood clots, carries an EGF exon (from epidermal growth factor), a “finger” exon (from fibronectin) and two “kringle” exons (from plasminogen).

How transposable elements contribute

Mobile DNA as an evolutionary engine.

Transposable elements promote recombination between chromosomes; inserted into a gene they can block it, inserted near one they can turn its expression up or down; and they can carry exons or whole genes to new places. Most such changes are harmful; a few are the stuff of new adaptations.

In primates, some Alu elements have been recruited as exons, adding new alternative splicing.

Takeaway · 18.5 (part 2)

  1. Globin families: one ancestor, duplicated 450–500 mya, diverged and moved.
  2. Duplicates can gain new functions (lysozyme → α-lactalbumin).
  3. Exon duplication and shuffling build new proteins (TPA).
18.6

Comparing genomes

Distantly related · Figure 18.15

A tree read from genes that barely change.

Comparing highly conserved genes reveals ancient splits: bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes diverged 2–4 billion years ago. The same conservation is why yeast, flies and mice (model organisms) can teach us about human biology.

Closely related · human and chimpanzee

1.2% and 2.7%.

Aligned, the human and chimp genomes differ by 1.2% at single base pairs and by 2.7% through insertions and deletions. Genes that changed faster in humans include ones for defence against malaria and tuberculosis, and for regulating brain size.

FOXP2 · Figure 18.16

A gene for a voice.

FOXP2 is a transcription factor that switches on genes involved in vocalization in vertebrates. Mutations in it cause severe speech and language problems in people; mice with both copies disrupted don't make the normal ultrasonic whistles.

Wild type · heterozygote · homozygote: the homozygous pups are silent.

Within a species

Humans are young, and alike.

Most human variation is single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), plus inversions, deletions and duplications, including many copy-number variants (CNVs), where a segment appears different numbers of times in different people.

Predict · Concept Check 18.6

Would a macaque's genome be more like a mouse's or a human's?

Right: macaques and humans are both primates; their lineages split far more recently than primates and rodents did.

The rule

The more recent the ancestor, the more alike the genome.

Takeaway · 18.6 (part 1)

  1. Conserved genes reveal ancient splits (2–4 bya).
  2. Human vs chimp: 1.2% substitutions, 2.7% indels.
  3. Human variation: mostly SNPs, plus CNVs.
18.6

Evo-devo

Evolutionary developmental biology

180 nucleotides in every animal.

Evo-devo compares developmental processes across organisms. Every Drosophila homeotic gene contains a 180-nucleotide sequence, the homeobox, which codes for a 60-amino-acid homeodomain: the part of the protein that binds DNA when it acts as a transcription factor.

Identical or very similar sequences exist in vertebrates, other invertebrates, yeasts and plants.

Figure 18.17 · the fruit fly

Chromosome order = body order.

The fly's Hox genes sit on its chromosome in the same order as the body regions they control, head to tail. Colours match each gene to the region where it is expressed: 10-hour embryo, then adult.

Predict

A mouse has Hox genes in the same order as the fly's, matching the same body regions. Why?

Right: the order and function were conserved for over half a billion years of separate evolution.

Figure 18.17 · the mouse

Four clusters, the same order.

The mouse has four Hox clusters on four chromosomes. Each follows the fly's order, and each gene is expressed along the 12-day embryo's head-to-tail axis where its fly counterpart is.

Figure 18.18 · crustacean vs insect

Same genes. Different places. Different animals.

A brine shrimp and a grasshopper share the same four Hox genes. In the crustacean they're expressed together all along the thorax: every segment grows a swimming limb. In the insect each has its own region, and in the abdomen they suppress legs.

Small changes in where and when genes are expressed, not new genes, can remodel a body.

Field note · Ciona robusta tadpole

Forty cells that make a backbone's ancestor.

The sea squirt's tadpole has a notochord of exactly 40 cells. They're specified by Brachyury, a transcription factor so conserved that mice missing one copy of it are born with short tails. Ciona is the closest living invertebrate relative of vertebrates: a chance to read the notochord's genetic program at its simplest.

Takeaway · 18.6 (part 2)

  1. Homeobox (180 nt) → homeodomain (60 aa), binds DNA.
  2. Hox genes: same order in fly and mouse, same axis, common ancestor.
  3. Changing where genes are expressed changes body plans.
