Overview · Mining the genome · Figure 18.1

A living fossil, read letter by letter.

The elephant shark (Callorhinchus milii) has a skeleton of cartilage and the slowest-changing genome of every vertebrate sequenced so far: a doorway into the distant past.

Its opposite: the tiger tail seahorse, whose males brood the young, has the fastest-evolving fish genome known. Comparing genomes like these is how we ask what made each species what it is.

Genome · genomics · bioinformatics

Everything it is, written in four letters.

A genome is an organism's complete set of genetic material. Genomics studies whole sets of genes and their interactions; bioinformatics applies computing to store and analyse the enormous data.

Watch the shark come apart into the one molecule that built it.

DNA · atom by atom

Two strands, one code.

Sugar–phosphate backbones outside (phosphorus orange, oxygen red); paired bases stacked inside: A–T, G–C. About 3.4 nm per turn, 2 nm across.

Rendered from atom positions, not drawn: every sphere here is one atom.

18.1

Reading the genome

1990 – 2003 · Human Genome Project

Twenty centres. Six countries. One book.

The Human Genome Project officially began in 1990 and was largely completed in 2003: an international, publicly funded consortium to sequence every base pair of a human genome.

Its biggest legacy was not the sequence but the machines it forced into existence: faster, cheaper sequencing.

3 billion base pairs

The helix coils into a chromosome.

A haploid set of human chromosomes holds about 3 billion base pairs. The goal of mapping any genome: the complete nucleotide sequence of every chromosome.

Shown here as a duplicated (metaphase) chromosome: two sister chromatids joined at the centromere, stained into bands.

Whole-genome shotgun · step 1 · Figure 18.2

Take many copies. Cut them at random.

In 1998 J. Craig Venter proposed the whole-genome shotgun approach: cut the DNA from many copies of a chromosome into overlapping fragments short enough to sequence.

Each copy breaks in different places, so the pieces overlap. That overlap is the whole trick.

Step 2 · clone

Clone every fragment.

The fragments are cloned in plasmids or other vectors, making enough of each to sequence.

Predict · the book's Visual Skills question

Why are the fragments shown scattered, not in order?

Right: the cut is random, so a fragment's position is lost. Only the overlaps between fragments can put the order back.

Step 3 · sequence

Read each fragment.

Every fragment is sequenced on its own. Three of them, straight from Figure 18.2:

CGCCATCAGTAGTCCGCTATACGAACGATACTGGT

Step 4 · order by overlap

Software stitches them back together.

Computer programs find where the end of one read matches the start of another, then line up thousands of overlapping reads into one continuous sequence.

…CGCCATCAGTCCGCTATACGATACTGGT…

Check · 18.1

Why cut many copies of the DNA instead of just one?

Right: every copy breaks in different places. Without overlapping fragments, software could never tell which piece follows which.

Faster, cheaper

From thirteen years to a single day.

A 1980s lab read about 1,000 base pairs a day; by 2000, 1,000 a second; by 2018 the best machines read nearly 35 million a second. Sequencing by synthesis reads millions of ~300-bp fragments at once, with no cloning step.

The first human genome: 13 years and $100 million. In 2007 one person's: 4 months, about $1 million. By 2018: a day or less, about $1,000.

Metagenomics

Sequence a whole community at once.

In metagenomics, DNA from every species in an environmental sample is collected and sequenced together; software sorts the partial sequences into individual genomes.

No need to grow each species in the lab: most microbes can't be cultured at all.

2018 · the contents of cow rumens

913 genomes out of one soup.

Researchers sequenced DNA from cow rumens and assembled 913 microbial genomes, finding over 60,000 genes for carbohydrate digestion that had never been identified.

Check · 18.1

What makes metagenomics different from sequencing one species?

Right: DNA from the whole sample is sequenced together and software sorts it into genomes, so microbes that can't be cultured can still be studied.

Takeaway · 18.1

  1. Shotgun: cut many copies at random, clone, sequence, order by overlaps.
  2. New methods sequence short fragments directly, millions at once.
  3. Metagenomics sequences a whole community's DNA together.
18.2

Bioinformatics

Centralized resources

Every genome, one search box.

The National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), created by the National Library of Medicine and the NIH, runs GenBank: a public database of every sequence submitted. Europe (EMBL), Japan (DDBJ) and China (BGI) keep matching archives.

Anyone can search it for a DNA sequence, a predicted protein, or a stretch of amino acids, and view solved 3-D protein structures.

Predict · your first bioinformatics move

You've sequenced a gene no one has studied. Fastest clue to its function?

Right: a program like BLAST aligns your sequence against millions of others. Similar sequence usually means similar function, because similar genes share ancestry.

BLAST · alignment

Line it up against everything ever sequenced.

BLAST slides your query along database sequences and scores the matches. A protein's predicted amino acid sequence is compared too: amino acids can match even where the DNA has drifted.

This is how most genes in a new genome are first given a likely function.

Understanding protein-coding genes

Where does a gene start?

Software scans a raw sequence for the signatures of genes: start and stop codons, RNA-splicing sites, and evidence that the region is actually transcribed into RNA.

A genome browser stacks this evidence as tracks: here, one gene's five exons with its RNA piling up over them.

Check · 18.2

Which is evidence that a stretch of DNA is a protein-coding gene?

Right: gene-finding software looks for exactly these signals, and RNA evidence shows the region is actually used.

Field note · Ciona robusta

Two sea squirts, one secret switch.

Compare the genomes of two Ciona species and most noncoding DNA has drifted apart, but some blocks stay nearly identical. Those conserved noncoding blocks often turn out to be enhancers: switches that control when a gene turns on.

That's how the notochord enhancer of Ciona's Brachyury gene was found and narrowed to 434 bp; each candidate is tested by electroporating reporter DNA into hundreds of embryos at once (Corbo, Levine & Zeller 1997).

Unknown genes

When nothing matches, break it.

For a gene with no known relative, researchers disable it and watch what goes wrong in the organism. Biochemical and functional studies fill in the rest.

The systems level

Genes work in networks.

Proteomics studies the full set of proteins a genome encodes (its proteome). Systems biology models the behaviour of whole systems from the interactions among their parts, like this map of yeast proteins, coloured by the cell function each cluster serves.

Check · 18.2

Systems biology tries to…

Right: it studies networks of genes and proteins as a whole. That's why a systems approach suits a disease like cancer, where many genes change together.

2003 · ENCODE

Most of the genome is read, somewhere, sometime.

The ENCODE project (Encyclopedia of DNA Elements) catalogued protein-coding genes, noncoding RNA genes and regulatory sequences. At least 75% of the human genome is transcribed at some point in at least one cell type.

It was extended to the nematode and the fruit fly (modENCODE).

Medicine

Cancer, read as a system.

The Cancer Genome Atlas compares the genomes of tumours with normal tissue, gene by gene, across many cancer types, finding shared networks of changes rather than single broken genes. Microarray chips test the expression of thousands of genes at once.

BLAST · query vs GenBank

Takeaway · 18.2

  1. Databases (GenBank) and tools (BLAST) let anyone compare sequences.
  2. Similar sequence suggests similar function; genes are found by their signals.
  3. Systems biology studies networks; ENCODE: ≥ 75% of DNA is transcribed.
18.3

Size is not complexity

Predict

Which has the largest genome?

Right: Paris japonica has about 149,000 Mb, roughly 50 times a human's 3,000 Mb.

Genome size · Table 18.1

Volume, drawn to scale.

Most bacteria and archaea have 1–6 million base pairs (Mb); most plants and animals over 100 Mb; humans about 3,000 Mb. Within each domain, genome size shows no systematic relationship to how complex the organism looks.

Each sphere's volume is proportional to its genome. The last one never fits the frame.

Predict

Who has more genes: you, or an ear of corn?

Right: corn has about 32,000 genes; humans fewer than 21,000, about as many as a 1-mm nematode worm.

Number of genes

More DNA doesn't mean more genes.

Free-living bacteria and archaea have 1,500–7,500 genes; unicellular fungi about 5,000; multicellular eukaryotes up to at least 40,000. The nematode (C. elegans, 100 Mb) has about 20,100 genes; the fruit fly (165 Mb) only about 14,000.

Gene density

A bacterium is almost all genes.

Each bar is one million base pairs. E. coli packs about 950 genes into it; a human, about 7. Mammals have the lowest gene density known: long introns and vast noncoding DNA between genes.

Alternative splicing

One gene, several proteins.

How do so few genes build a human? In vertebrates most genes can be spliced more than one way: alternative splicing keeps different combinations of exons, so one gene yields several different polypeptides.

Check · 18.3

Humans have fewer than 21,000 genes but make far more kinds of polypeptides. The main reason?

Right: one gene can be spliced several ways, so vertebrates get more than one polypeptide from many genes.

Takeaway · 18.3

  1. Genome size doesn't track complexity; gene number doesn't track size.
  2. Humans: ~3,000 Mb, < 21,000 genes, ~7 genes per Mb.
  3. Alternative splicing gets many proteins from one gene.
18.4

What a genome holds

The human genome, end to end

Three billion letters, laid out.

Every colour is a kind of DNA sequence, in its true proportion of the human genome. Multicellular eukaryotes carry far more noncoding DNA than bacteria do, and far more repeats.

Predict

How much of it codes for proteins?

Right: exons are about 1.5%. The other 98.5% doesn't code for proteins, rRNAs or tRNAs.

Types of DNA sequences · Figure 18.5

1.5% codes for protein.

Only the exons are protein-coding. The rest was once dismissed as “junk DNA”, but much of it is conserved across species and much of it is transcribed, which argues strongly that it matters.

Sequences that natural selection keeps unchanged over millions of years are almost certainly doing something.

Introns and regulatory sequences · about a quarter

Inside genes and beside them.

Introns (about 20%) sit inside genes and are spliced out of the RNA. Regulatory sequences (about 5%) such as enhancers and promoters control when and where each gene is transcribed. Together, gene-related noncoding DNA is about a quarter of the genome.

Intergenic DNA · pseudogenes · unique noncoding DNA

The DNA between genes.

Noncoding DNA between genes is intergenic DNA. Some of it is pseudogenes: former genes that have accumulated mutations over time and no longer produce a working product. About 15% is unique noncoding DNA, present in a single copy, whose function is mostly unknown.

Check · 18.4

What is a pseudogene?

Right: a pseudogene is the fossil of a gene. It still looks like one, but mutations have disabled it.

Repetitive DNA · about half the genome

Over half of us is repeats.

Repetitive DNA is any sequence present in multiple copies. About three-quarters of it is transposable elements and related sequences: 44% of the whole genome. The other 14% arose from mistakes during DNA replication or recombination.

Simple sequence DNA · short tandem repeats

Stutters that make you unique.

Simple sequence DNA is many copies of a short unit in tandem, like GTTACGTTACGTTAC… When the unit is 2–5 nucleotides it's a short tandem repeat (STR). The number of repeats at a site varies from person to person, so a set of STRs gives each person a unique genetic profile.

Check · 18.4

Why are STRs so useful for identifying people?

Right: two people rarely share the same repeat counts at many STR sites at once, which is why forensic labs compare them.

Where the repeats sit

Structure, and copies of copies.

Much simple sequence DNA sits at centromeres and telomeres, where it plays a structural role: organising chromatin and protecting chromosome ends. About a third of the 14% is large-segment duplications (5–6%): long stretches copied from one chromosomal region to another.

Next · the notebook

The biggest slice moves.

That 44% is DNA that copies itself from place to place. The first person to see it happen did so decades before anyone knew genes were made of DNA.

Takeaway · 18.4 (part 1)

  1. Exons: 1.5%; introns + regulatory: ~¼; the rest intergenic.
  2. Repetitive DNA ≈ half; transposable elements ≈ 44%.
  3. STRs (2–5-nt units) vary between people: genetic profiles.
18.4

Jumping genes

Barbara McClintock · Indian corn · Figure 18.6

Speckled kernels, moving genes.

Breeding corn in the 1940s and '50s, Barbara McClintock saw kernel colours change in ways that only made sense if pieces of DNA were moving into and out of the genes for pigment. She was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1983.

An element that jumps into a pigment gene switches it off; where it jumps back out in one cell, colour returns in that cell's descendants: a spot.

Check · 18.4

What explained the mottled kernels?

Right: transposable elements. When one sits inside a pigment gene the gene is disrupted; when it leaves, colour comes back.

Into one kernel

Transposable elements.

Transposable elements are stretches of DNA that can move from one site in a cell's DNA to another. They're found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and they come in two kinds, told apart by how they move.

Transposons · cut and paste · Figure 18.7

Cut it out, paste it in.

A transposon moves through a DNA intermediate. An enzyme, transposase, which the transposon itself usually encodes, cuts it out of its old site and inserts it at a new one. The gap left behind is repaired.

Transposons · copy and paste

Or copy it, and paste the copy.

Some transposons are replicated first: the original stays where it was and the new copy is inserted somewhere else. Either way, the element is DNA the whole time.

Wherever it lands, it can disrupt a gene or change how nearby genes are expressed.

Predict · Concept Check

Which kind of element always leaves a copy at the original site?

Right: a retrotransposon is copied into RNA first; the original never leaves, and a new DNA copy is inserted elsewhere.

Retrotransposons · Figure 18.8

Through RNA and back.

A retrotransposon is transcribed into an RNA intermediate. Reverse transcriptase, which the retrotransposon encodes, copies that RNA back into DNA, and the new DNA copy inserts at another site. The original never leaves.

That's why they pile up: every jump adds a copy. Most of the 44% is retrotransposons.

Check · 18.4

What does reverse transcriptase make?

Right: it runs the usual flow backwards, RNA → DNA. That's what lets a retrotransposon's RNA become a new DNA copy.

Alu and L1

A tenth of you is one sequence.

Alu elements, about 300 nucleotides long, make up about 10% of the human genome; many are transcribed into RNA, though any function is unknown. L1 (LINE-1) retrotransposons are about 17%; they rarely move, and may help regulate gene expression.

Why they matter

Parasites, or raw material?

Transposable elements can break genes, switch them on or off, and shuffle DNA around. Mostly harmful, occasionally useful: in 18.5 they turn out to be one of the engines of genome evolution.

Takeaway · 18.4 (part 2)

  1. Transposons move by a DNA intermediate (transposase).
  2. Retrotransposons move by an RNA intermediate (reverse transcriptase) and always leave a copy.
  3. Alu ≈ 10%, L1 ≈ 17% of the human genome.
18.4

Gene families

Identical genes · Figure 18.9a

Hundreds of copies, side by side.

Most genes are single-copy, but multigene families are collections of two or more identical or very similar genes. The ribosomal RNA genes repeat in tandem, hundreds to thousands of times. Each unit is transcribed into one RNA that is cut into 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs; units are separated by nontranscribed spacer DNA.

Each “tree” is one unit being transcribed by many RNA polymerases at once: the longest branches are the most complete transcripts.

Check · 18.4

Why keep so many identical rRNA genes?

Right: rRNA is the main component of ribosomes and isn't translated, so the only way to make it faster is more genes transcribing at once.

Nonidentical genes · Figure 18.9b

Globins: two families, two chromosomes.

The α-globin family sits on chromosome 16, the β-globin family on chromosome 11. Members of each family are similar but not identical, and are expressed at different times in development. Scattered among the working genes are pseudogenes (ψ).

Check · 18.4

Where are the α- and β-globin genes?

Right: α family on 16, β family on 11. Each family is a cluster of related genes and pseudogenes.

Where families come from

Copies that drifted apart.

Nonidentical families arise when a gene is duplicated and the copies then accumulate different mutations. Some copies gain slightly different jobs; some lose function and become pseudogenes. In 18.5 you'll watch the globin families grow from a single ancestor.

Hemoglobin

Two α-type chains, two β-type chains.

Each hemoglobin molecule is four polypeptides: two from the α family and two from the β family. Each chain holds an iron-containing heme group, which binds O₂.

Predict

A fetus uses γ-globin instead of β. Why would it need a different hemoglobin?

Right: embryonic and fetal hemoglobins bind O₂ more strongly than adult hemoglobin, so oxygen moves from mother to fetus across the placenta.

A developmental switch

Different genes, different times.

ζ (α family) and ε (β family) are made in the embryo. In the fetus, α and the two γ genes (Gγ, Aγ). In the adult, α and β, with a little δ. Hemoglobin is always two α-type plus two β-type chains; which members fill those places changes as you develop.

Curves are schematic: the order and timing of the switches, not exact amounts.

Why families are worth having

One job, tuned for each stage.

A family lets a genome make many copies of one product (rRNA) or slightly different versions of it for different circumstances (globins). Both began the same way: with a duplication.

Takeaway · 18.4 (part 3)

  1. Multigene families: identical (rRNA, in tandem) or nonidentical (globins).
  2. α-globins on chromosome 16, β-globins on 11, with pseudogenes.
  3. Family members are expressed at different developmental stages.
18.5

Duplication and rearrangement

How genomes grow

Mutation is the raw material.

The earliest life probably had the minimum number of genes it needed to survive and reproduce. Over time, genomes grew. The extra DNA, made by duplications, was the raw material from which new genes diversified.

Duplication of entire chromosome sets

An accident that doubles everything.

An error in meiosis can leave a cell with one or more extra sets of chromosomes: polyploidy. Because one copy of each gene still does the essential job, the spare copy is free to mutate. Most such changes are useless, but occasionally one gives a new function.

Check · 18.5

Why can a duplicated gene evolve a new function?

Right: with one copy covering the essential function, mutations in the spare copy aren't removed by selection, so it can drift toward something new.

Field note · Ciona robusta

One copy where we have four.

Early vertebrates went through two rounds of whole-genome duplication. Ciona, whose lineage split off before them, keeps many developmental genes in single copy: one Hox cluster (split across two chromosomes) where mammals have four, and one Brachyury.

That's part of why it's such a clean system for studying how a gene like Brachyury works (Dehal & Boore 2005).

Predict

Humans have 23 chromosome pairs; chimpanzees have 24. What most likely happened?

Right: human chromosome 2 matches chimp chromosomes 12 and 13 end to end, and still carries the evidence of the join.

Alterations of chromosome structure · Figure 18.10

A fusion you can still see.

After the human and chimp lineages split, two ancestral chromosomes fused end to end. Human chromosome 2 still has telomere-like sequences in its middle, where two chromosome ends joined, and the remains of a second centromere, now inactive.

Figure 18.11 · human vs mouse

Same blocks, reshuffled.

Large blocks of genes on human chromosome 16 are found together on mouse chromosomes 7, 8, 16 and 17. The genes inside each block stayed together, while the chromosomes around them were broken and rejoined in each lineage.

Rearrangements and new species

Broken, flipped, rejoined.

Duplications and inversions arise from errors in meiotic recombination. Their rate seems to have sped up about 100 million years ago, around when mammals diversified. Rearrangements can help new species form: chromosomes that no longer match can't pair properly at meiosis in hybrids.

The breakpoints tend to fall in “hot spots”, often near duplicated sequences.

Figure 18.12 · unequal crossing over

A misaligned swap copies a gene.

During prophase I, homologous chromosomes can pair slightly out of register, often where transposable elements give them matching sequence in the wrong place. A crossover there is unequal: one chromatid ends up with two copies of a gene, the other with none.

Other ways to duplicate DNA

Slips and swaps.

Errors during DNA replication can also duplicate DNA: the template strand can slip, so a stretch is copied twice. Unequal crossing over and replication errors both produce duplications of gene-sized regions, the starting point for new genes.

Check · 18.5

Unequal crossing over produces…

Right: what one chromatid gains, the other loses.

Next

A spare copy is an experiment.

Takeaway · 18.5 (part 1)

  1. Polyploidy duplicates whole sets; spare copies can diverge.
  2. Human 2 = a fusion of two ancestral (chimp 12 + 13) chromosomes.
  3. Unequal crossing over: one chromatid gains a gene, the other loses it.
18.5

New genes from old

Genes with related functions

Read the globin families backwards.

Every α and β gene, pseudogenes included, descends from one ancestor. Watch the maps fold back into a family tree.

Figure 18.13 · 450–500 million years

Duplicate. Mutate. Move. Repeat.

An ancestral globin gene duplicated about 450–500 million years ago. Both copies accumulated mutations, becoming the α and β lineages; transposition carried them to different chromosomes; further duplications and mutations gave today's families.

Check · 18.5

How did the α and β lineages end up on different chromosomes?

Right: after the first duplication, a copy was carried to another chromosome by transposition. The families then grew in place.

Reading the tree

Similarity tells time.

The more alike two globin genes are in sequence, the more recently they were duplicated. Pseudogenes are copies that mutated until they stopped working.

The book's Scientific Skills Exercise does exactly this with an amino acid identity table of human globins.

Genes with novel functions

Same shape, new job.

Lysozyme is an enzyme that helps protect animals against bacteria. α-Lactalbumin is a nonenzymatic protein that helps make milk in mammals. They're so similar in sequence and 3-D structure that they must be duplicated copies of one ancestral gene; one copy gained a new function.

Predict · Test Your Understanding

Two proteins share one domain but are otherwise unrelated. Most likely cause?

Right: domains are often encoded by separate exons, so an exon moved into another gene brings its domain with it.

Rearrangements of parts of genes

Proteins built from parts.

Proteins often have domains, discrete structural and functional regions, frequently encoded by different exons. Unequal crossing over within a gene can duplicate an exon (exon duplication), giving a protein a second copy of a domain.

Figure 18.14 · exon shuffling

TPA: a gene assembled from three others.

In exon shuffling, exons are mixed and matched between different genes. The gene for tissue plasminogen activator (TPA), a protein that dissolves blood clots, carries an EGF exon (from epidermal growth factor), a “finger” exon (from fibronectin) and two “kringle” exons (from plasminogen).

Check · 18.5

Where did TPA's two kringle exons come from?

Right: a kringle exon came from plasminogen and was duplicated. Fibronectin gave the finger, EGF the growth-factor exon.

How transposable elements contribute

Mobile DNA as an evolutionary engine.

Transposable elements promote recombination between chromosomes. Inserted into a coding sequence they can block a protein; inserted into a regulatory sequence they can raise or lower expression. They can carry exons or whole genes to new places. Most such changes are harmful; a few are the stuff of new adaptations.

In primates

Alu, recruited.

Some Alu elements inside genes have become new exons, adding alternative splicing and new versions of proteins.

Takeaway · 18.5 (part 2)

  1. Globin families: one ancestor, duplicated 450–500 mya, diverged and moved.
  2. Duplicates can gain new functions (lysozyme → α-lactalbumin).
  3. Exon duplication and shuffling build new proteins (TPA).
18.6

Comparing genomes

Distantly related species · Figure 18.15

A tree read from genes that barely change.

Comparing highly conserved genes, ones whose sequences have changed very little, reveals ancient splits: bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes diverged 2–4 billion years ago.

What comparisons tell us

Old events, recent events.

Distantly related species show ancient events; closely related species show recent ones. The same conservation explains why model organisms (yeast, flies, mice) can teach us about human biology: many of our genes have close relatives in theirs.

Closely related species · human and chimpanzee

1.2% and 2.7%.

Aligned, the human and chimp genomes differ by 1.2% at single base pairs and by 2.7% through insertions and deletions of larger stretches. Genes that changed faster in humans include ones for defence against malaria and tuberculosis, and for regulating brain size.

Since the two lineages split, Alu elements have made about three times as many new copies of themselves in humans as in chimps.

Check · 18.6

How much do human and chimp genomes differ?

Right: 1.2% substitutions and 2.7% indels. Small differences, but in the right genes they matter.

FOXP2 · Figure 18.16

A gene for a voice.

FOXP2 codes for a transcription factor that switches on genes involved in vocalization in vertebrates. It has evolved faster in the human lineage. Mutations in it cause severe speech and language problems in people; mice with both copies disrupted don't make the normal ultrasonic whistles.

Wild type · heterozygote · homozygote: the homozygous pups are silent.

Check · 18.6

What happens to mouse pups with both copies of FOXP2 disrupted?

Right: evidence that FOXP2 is needed for vocalization, and a clue to why its changes in humans may matter for speech.

Within a species

Humans are young, and alike.

Our species is young, so humans vary little. Most variation is single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), plus inversions, deletions and duplications, including many copy-number variants (CNVs), where a segment appears different numbers of times in different people.

Why variation matters

Markers for disease and ancestry.

SNPs, CNVs and STRs act as genetic markers: comparing them across many people helps locate genes involved in disease and reconstruct the history of human populations.

Predict · Concept Check 18.6

Would a macaque's genome be more like a mouse's or a human's?

Right: macaques and humans are both primates; their lineages split far more recently than primates and rodents did.

The rule

The more recent the ancestor, the more alike the genome.

Takeaway · 18.6 (part 1)

  1. Conserved genes reveal ancient splits (2–4 bya).
  2. Human vs chimp: 1.2% substitutions, 2.7% indels.
  3. Human variation: mostly SNPs, plus CNVs.
18.6

Evo-devo

Evolutionary developmental biology

180 nucleotides in every animal.

Evo-devo compares developmental processes across organisms. Homeotic genes are master regulators that control the identity of body parts. Every Drosophila homeotic gene contains a 180-nucleotide sequence, the homeobox, which codes for a 60-amino-acid homeodomain: the part of the protein that binds DNA when it acts as a transcription factor.

Identical or very similar sequences exist in vertebrates, other invertebrates, yeasts and plants.

Check · 18.6

What does the homeobox code for?

Right: the homeodomain lets the protein bind DNA and act as a transcription factor, switching on the genes that build a body region.

Figure 18.17 · the fruit fly

Chromosome order = body order.

The fly's Hox genes sit on its chromosome in the same order as the body regions they control, head to tail. Colours match each gene to the region where it is expressed: the 10-hour embryo, then the adult.

Predict

A mouse has Hox genes in the same order as the fly's, matching the same body regions. Why?

Right: the order and function were conserved for over half a billion years of separate evolution.

Figure 18.17 · the mouse

Four clusters, the same order.

The mouse has four Hox clusters on four chromosomes. Each follows the fly's order, and each gene is expressed along the 12-day embryo's head-to-tail axis where its fly counterpart is.

Concept Check · then why so different?

Same toolkit, different instructions.

If flies and mice share homeobox genes, why don't they look alike? Because the genes differ in when and where they're expressed, and in which genes their proteins switch on. The homeodomain is nearly the same; the rest of each protein, and its targets, differ.

Figure 18.18 · crustacean vs insect

Same genes. Different places. Different animals.

A brine shrimp and a grasshopper share the same four Hox genes. In the crustacean they're expressed together all along the thorax, and every segment grows a swimming limb. In the insect each has its own region, and in the abdomen they suppress legs.

Small changes in where and when genes are expressed, not new genes, can remodel a body.

Check · 18.6

A brine shrimp and a grasshopper have the same Hox genes. What makes their bodies differ?

Right: same genes, different expression domains, different animals.

Field note · Ciona robusta tadpole

Forty cells that make a backbone's ancestor.

The sea squirt's tadpole has a notochord of exactly 40 cells, specified by Brachyury: a transcription factor so conserved that mice missing one copy of it are born with short tails. Tunicates like Ciona are the closest living invertebrate relatives of vertebrates, a chance to read the notochord's genetic program at its simplest.

Takeaway · 18.6 (part 2)

  1. Homeobox (180 nt) → homeodomain (60 aa), binds DNA.
  2. Hox genes: same order in fly and mouse, same axis, common ancestor.
  3. Changing where genes are expressed changes body plans.

The whole-genome shotgun approach puts sequenced fragments in order by…

Software finds where the end of one read matches the start of another and stitches the reads into one continuous sequence.

Sequencing a human genome took 13 years and $100 million the first time. By 2018 it took…

Faster methods such as sequencing by synthesis read millions of short fragments at once.

03 · 18.2

What is BLAST used for?

Similar sequences usually mean shared ancestry and a similar function, so BLAST is usually the first clue to what a new gene does.

04 · 18.2

The ENCODE project found that at least this much of the human genome is transcribed at some point, in at least one cell type:

Far more of the genome is transcribed than codes for protein, which suggests much noncoding DNA has roles.

05 · 18.3

Which statement about genome size is true?

Paris japonica has about 50 times more DNA than a human; corn has more genes than we do.

06 · 18.3

Why do humans have such a low gene density (about 7 genes per Mb)?

E. coli packs about 950 genes into each million base pairs; mammals spread theirs out among vast stretches of noncoding DNA.

07 · 18.4

About what percentage of the human genome is exons that code for protein?

The other 98.5% doesn't code for proteins, rRNAs or tRNAs.

08 · 18.4

Transposable elements and related sequences make up about how much of the human genome?

Mostly retrotransposons: L1 (about 17%) and Alu (about 10%).

09 · 18.4

How does a retrotransposon differ from a cut-and-paste transposon?

The RNA copy is turned back into DNA by reverse transcriptase and inserted elsewhere; the original stays put.

10 · 18.4

The α-globin and β-globin gene families are found on…

α family on chromosome 16, β family on chromosome 11, each with working genes and pseudogenes.

11 · 18.4

Which β-family genes are expressed in the fetus?

ε is embryonic; the two γ genes are fetal; β (and a little δ) are adult. ζ is an α-family gene.

12 · 18.5

What is the evidence that human chromosome 2 formed by a fusion?

Those are the scars of two chromosome ends joining. Chimps still carry the two as separate chromosomes (12 and 13).

13 · 18.5

Unequal crossing over is often made possible by…

Matching transposable elements in the wrong places let homologs misalign; the crossover then leaves one chromatid with a duplication and the other with a deletion.

14 · 18.5

The α- and β-globin families arose from an ancestral globin gene that duplicated about…

Duplication, then mutation, then transposition to different chromosomes, then further duplications.

15 · 18.5

Lysozyme and α-lactalbumin are similar in sequence and 3-D structure but have different jobs. This shows that…

After duplication, one copy kept the enzyme's job while the other became a milk protein in mammals.

16 · 18.5

The TPA gene contains exons that came from which genes?

An EGF exon, a finger exon from fibronectin, and two kringle exons from plasminogen: exon shuffling.

17 · 18.6

Comparing highly conserved genes is most useful for…

Genes that barely change still hold a readable signal after billions of years.

18 · 18.6

Most genetic variation between individual humans is in the form of…

Plus inversions, deletions and duplications, including many copy-number variants.

19 · 18.6

The homeobox is a 180-nucleotide sequence that codes for…

The homeodomain lets homeotic proteins act as transcription factors.

20 · 18.6

Flies and mice share Hox genes in the same order, yet look nothing alike. The best explanation is that…

Same toolkit, different instructions. The same point explains brine shrimp vs grasshopper.
